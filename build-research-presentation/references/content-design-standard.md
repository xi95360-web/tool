# Content and Design Standard

## 1. The Scientific Unit of a Slide

Build each content slide around:

`question or claim → evidence → interpretation → limitation → next question`

The audience should identify within five seconds:

1. what the slide asks or concludes;
2. where to look;
3. what observation matters;
4. how strongly that observation supports the claim.

Use annotations, direct labels, restrained highlighting, and claim titles to expose this structure.

The action title and dominant exhibit must agree: the title states the bounded takeaway, while the exhibit makes the observation visible. If either can be removed without weakening the slide, redesign the slide.

## 2. Evidence Status

Use exactly these evidence statuses:

- `PUBLISHED`: verified published or archived evidence;
- `USER_DATA`: user-provided unpublished result;
- `PROPOSED`: future design, hypothesis, prediction, or proposed model;
- `INFERENCE`: interpretation not directly demonstrated;
- `EVIDENCE_GAP`: unresolved or inaccessible support.

Conceptual model, figure, table, and schematic are content types and inherit one of the statuses above. Show the status in text or a stable badge; color may reinforce it but cannot be the only cue.

Do not make predicted plots look like measured data. Prefer a decision table or explicitly labeled schematic for proposed outcomes.

## 3. Figure Adaptation

Paper figures are designed for repeated close reading; presentation figures are seen briefly. Therefore:

- select only the panels needed for the spoken claim;
- preserve values, uncertainty, axes, groups, and scientific meaning;
- enlarge fonts and line weights;
- remove irrelevant legends or decorative detail;
- directly label curves or conditions;
- highlight the compared groups;
- redraw mechanisms and workflows when necessary;
- state “adapted from” and cite the original.

Never crop away a control, axis, baseline, or condition in a way that changes interpretation. Never beautify by changing data.

Maintain a transformation log for every adapted quantitative figure:

| Output slide/object | Original source + panel | Transformation | Data extraction method | Values checked against original? | Reviewer |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---|
|  |  | crop/redraw/relabel/recolor | source data/digitized/manual | yes/no |  |

Compare the source and adaptation side by side. If values cannot be obtained and checked faithfully, preserve the original quantitative marks and limit changes to non-data annotations or do not redraw.

For each quantitative figure, be ready to explain:

- experimental unit and sample size;
- independent versus technical replicates;
- central tendency and error representation;
- statistical test and multiple-comparison treatment;
- control group and normalization;
- axis scale, units, exclusions, and missing data;
- effect size and uncertainty, not only significance.

For images or spectra, explain acquisition, representative-selection logic, scale bar/units, preprocessing, and quantitative support.

For chemical structures and mechanisms, redraw in an appropriate scientific editor when possible. Use consistent bond width, font weight, arrow style, atom labels, and object scale.

## 4. Text

- Use short phrases on the slide and full reasoning in notes.
- Avoid paragraphs and copied abstracts.
- Treat roughly 40 body-text words as an audit trigger, not a rigid limit; split the slide or move detail to notes/backup when the audience would need to read instead of listen.
- Use one to three concise annotations for the main visual.
- Define abbreviations on first use.
- Maintain consistent nomenclature and symbols.
- Put the conclusion close to the relevant evidence.
- Do not put the speech transcript on the slide.

Minimum readable sizes depend on room and template. For 16:9 academic content slides, use approximately 28–34 pt bold action titles, 20–24 pt body text, 16–18 pt figure labels, and 11–14 pt citations. Use 36–44 pt for short section/title statements. Keep action titles to one or two lines and never shrink them below 24 pt to rescue an overcrowded slide. Increase sizes for large rooms.

## 5. Layout and Visual System

- Use one dominant visual and a clear reading order.
- Allow multiple panels only when they act as one comparison or inferential unit.
- Preserve substantial whitespace, but avoid meaningless empty areas caused by poor alignment.
- Keep margins, grids, object scales, arrows, and text styles consistent.
- Use high contrast and a color-blind-safe palette.
- Default to a white or very light content background, one sans-serif family, and no more than three semantic colors; an institutional template may override these defaults.
- Reserve one accent color for evidence emphasis and another only if a stable semantic distinction requires it.
- Left-align body text by default. Choose evidence-left/interpretation-right, full-width exhibit, or another reading order according to the scientific content rather than enforcing one layout everywhere.
- Avoid rainbow palettes, shadows, excessive gradients, and decorative icons.
- Vary layouts according to scientific need, not for novelty.
- Use animation only to reveal a causal, temporal, or inferential sequence.

## 6. Citation Standard

Use compact on-slide citations and a complete backup bibliography.

For papers, include author, venue, year, and DOI when space permits. For data repositories, include the database, record/accession, access date if relevant, and original study. For adapted figures, cite the exact figure or panel. For user data, use experiment or file identifiers.

Verify:

- title, authors, year, venue, volume/pages or article number;
- DOI/PMID/accession;
- whether the cited source actually supports the local claim;
- whether a secondary source should be replaced by the original paper;
- peer-review status and corrections/retractions.

Do not republish or distribute figures beyond the permissions applicable to the user's venue. Preserve attribution and copyright notices where required.

## 7. Narrative and Timing

Start with the problem, establish only the background needed for the question, and make the gap explicit. Spend the largest share of the talk on evidence and experimental logic. End by answering the opening question.

Transitions must state the relationship:

- “This establishes X; it does not explain Y, so the next experiment tests…”
- “Because alternative A remains possible, the authors next use…”
- “Aim 1 supplies the parameter required to decide between the designs in Aim 2.”

For a fixed presentation slot, plan to finish slightly early and protect discussion time. Cut claims or move details to backup; do not compress the conclusion.

## 8. Forbidden Shortcuts

- fabricated or unresolved citations;
- decorative scientific images presented as evidence;
- unreadable screenshots of complete paper figures;
- unsupported causal language;
- journal prestige used as the main quality argument;
- “novel,” “important,” or “transformative” without a comparison or measurable consequence;
- generic slides containing only “Background,” “Methods,” or “Future work”;
- an empty “Thank you” or decorative section divider when a conclusion, roadmap, or scientific question can remain visible.
- topic-label titles such as “Background,” “Methods,” or “Results” when an evidence-bounded action title is possible.
