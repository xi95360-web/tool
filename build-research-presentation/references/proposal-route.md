# Proposal Route

## Purpose

Make a proposal presentation demonstrate that:

1. the problem matters;
2. the gap is real and precisely bounded;
3. the hypothesis or objective follows from evidence;
4. the aims can test it;
5. the plan is feasible;
6. failure modes are understood;
7. outcomes will change a scientific decision.

## Recommended Narrative

Adjust slide count to time; do not force one slide per item.

1. **Title / central question** — include the hypothesis, system, and scope.
2. **Unmet need with magnitude** — quantify the scientific, clinical, or technical problem.
3. **What is established** — show the minimum evidence needed to orient the audience.
4. **What remains unresolved** — contrast competing explanations or missing capability.
5. **Conceptual model** — connect prior evidence to the central hypothesis.
6. **Central hypothesis and falsifiable predictions** — state outcomes that could refute it.
7. **Aim map** — show how aims relate, what is independent, and what depends on prior results.
8. **Preliminary evidence / feasibility** — identify provenance and limits.
9. **Aim 1 logic and design**.
10. **Aim 1 expected outcomes and decision rule**.
11. **Aim 2 logic and design**.
12. **Aim 2 expected outcomes and decision rule**.
13. **Additional aims only if justified**.
14. **Rigor and analysis** — controls, blinding/randomization where relevant, sample-size logic, statistics, inclusion/exclusion, replication, data management.
15. **Risks and alternatives** — pair each high-risk assumption with a trigger and concrete fallback.
16. **Timeline, milestones, and go/no-go points**.
17. **Innovation and impact** — specify what capability, inference, or decision changes.
18. **Synthesis / ask** — answer the opening problem and state what success enables.
19. **Backup** — detailed methods, power calculations, reagent validation, sensitivity analyses, resources, alternative aims, and anticipated questions.

## Aim Contract

For every aim, specify:

- question;
- rationale and link to the central hypothesis;
- biological/chemical/physical system;
- independent variable or intervention;
- comparator and positive/negative controls;
- primary and secondary outcomes;
- measurement and analysis;
- sample-size or information-content rationale;
- expected outcomes under competing hypotheses;
- success/failure threshold;
- risk, trigger, and alternative;
- deliverable and downstream decision.

An aim fails if it is merely a method, contains multiple unrelated questions, depends completely on an earlier aim without fallback, or cannot alter the central conclusion.

Proposed controls and mechanisms are designs, not claims that they already exist. Label them `PROPOSED`. Source or obtain from the user every dose, effect size, assay performance value, variance, power input, recruitment rate, cost, and feasibility estimate. If unknown, state the unknown, show a sensitivity range where useful, and make obtaining the value a pilot milestone.

## When There Are No Preliminary Data

Do not manufacture feasibility or prematurely lock in a mechanism.

1. Establish feasibility from verified literature, available resources, validated assays, and explicit pilot milestones.
2. Narrow the system to one candidate class, one primary model, one primary endpoint, and a clear selection rule.
3. Use an exploratory/discovery aim only if the assignment or funding context permits it. Label it as discovery, not hypothesis confirmation.
4. If no candidate exists, a defensible staged plan may be:
   - Aim 1: identify and independently validate a candidate using preregistered ranking criteria;
   - Aim 2: test candidate-specific causality with perturbation, rescue, and confound controls;
   - mechanism work: a conditional branch triggered only by a reproducible functional effect.
5. Distinguish a biological null result from a technically uninformative failure with positive controls and decision rules.

Hold the finished deck if neither literature nor resources establish that the proposed measurement is feasible, the gap is real, or the discovery stage can produce a testable candidate within the available time.

## Ethics, Governance, and Rights

When applicable, show:

- human-subject approval, consent, privacy, and data-governance constraints;
- animal welfare and design implications;
- biosafety, dual-use, and material-transfer constraints;
- confidentiality and unpublished-data authorization;
- figure, software, dataset, and sample rights.

Unknown approval or permission status is not a low-risk default. Keep it unresolved and do not imply authorization.

## Evidence Language

Use calibrated verbs:

- published or preliminary data **show**, **estimate**, or **are consistent with**;
- the proposal **hypothesizes**, **predicts**, or **will test**;
- a future experiment cannot “demonstrate” a result before it is performed.

Avoid promising certainty. Show the informative result under success, null, and technically failed scenarios.

## Proposal-Specific Visuals

Prefer:

- quantitative unmet-need chart;
- gap/decision diagram;
- causal or mechanistic model;
- aim dependency map;
- experiment–readout–decision schematic;
- outcome matrix for competing hypotheses;
- preliminary data with controls;
- milestone and risk table.

Do not use stock photos to stand in for significance or innovation.

## Questions the Deck Must Survive

- What exact observation motivated the hypothesis?
- What result would falsify it?
- Why is each aim necessary?
- Are aims independent enough to survive partial failure?
- Does the assay measure the claimed construct?
- Are controls, replication, and statistics adequate?
- What is the largest feasibility risk?
- What will be learned if the expected result is absent?
- How does the outcome change the field, model, or next decision?
