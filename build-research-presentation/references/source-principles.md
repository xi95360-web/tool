## Assignment and Literature Research

- **pp. 5–6:** Start from the assigned topic and guiding questions. Cover them comprehensively, but adjust order and depth according to scientific logic. Keep within 15–20 minutes and revise the deck after instructor/peer feedback.
- **p. 9:** Match the information source to the question. Specific questions call for literature or database searches; broad learning may begin with reviews or books; latest developments require current journals.
- **p. 10:** Common evidence sources include research/review journals, scholarly books, theses, patents, preprints, and scientific databases. Locate the original source rather than stopping at a database result.
- **p. 11:** Search a topic with multiple keyword formulations and conceptual synonyms. This is especially useful for current questions and specific papers.
- **p. 12:** For foundational questions, begin with rational analysis, identify the governing variable, obtain primary quantitative data, and build the argument from those data rather than from unsupported description.
- **pp. 13–19:** The course illustrates general and specialized databases. Treat the named services as historical examples and verify current access and coverage before use.
- **pp. 20–24:** Distinguish research journals from review/commentary venues and use standardized journal names/abbreviations.
- **p. 21:** A conventional chemistry citation includes journal abbreviation, year, volume, and pages; online-first work may instead use DOI and publication status.
- **pp. 25–26:** Seek authoritative and representative material. Journal reputation and impact factor may help orient a search but should not replace expert judgment or paper-level evaluation.

## Content Planning

- **p. 28:** Audience determines detail. A specialist academic talk emphasizes research details; reporting/assessment contexts emphasize significance, outcomes, and the needs of supervisors, committees, or collaborators.
- **p. 29:** Never begin with unexplained detail. Introduce the topic, key question, and importance. Arrange multiple parts by logical relationship and create explicit transitions so listeners can follow the reasoning.
- **p. 30:** Use roughly 10–15% for background in the course's seminar example. Distill the topic's significance into one sentence, expand only what is needed, and distinguish overview from detailed examples.
- **p. 31:** Use roughly 60–70% for detailed treatment. Build a clear outline, select representative subtopics, and decide deliberately what to cover briefly versus deeply.
- **p. 32:** Avoid an unranked list, excessive experimental detail, missing hierarchy, weak transitions, and disconnected sections. A strong report has both logic and story, using the research process to carry academic information.
- **p. 33:** Use about 10% for synthesis: restate the main conclusions and optionally discuss unresolved problems. A conventional ending is brief; avoid awkward formulas such as “感谢聆听.”

## Slide Construction

- **p. 34:** Adapt density to the presentation type. Specialist talks may contain more detail and smaller elements than reporting talks, but both must remain readable. Account for the chosen aspect ratio.
- **pp. 35–36:** Use serious, readable sans-serif typography: Heiti/YaHei for Chinese and Arial/Helvetica for English. Use bold titles, avoid italics and decorative fonts. The course suggests Chinese page titles ≥28 pt and subtitles ≥24 pt; English titles ≥20 pt and subtitles ≥18 pt. Use strong red or blue sparingly for emphasis and check projector visibility.
- **p. 37:** Do not overcrowd pages or rely on pure text. Balance image and text, keep text items short (normally no more than two lines), and ensure figures reveal key details.
- **p. 38:** Animation is justified only when it exposes a reasoning process. Avoid distracting motion, jumping, or spinning; simple fades and sequential extension may support logic.
- **pp. 39–41:** Paper figures are not automatically presentation figures. Enlarge fonts and line weights, redraw chemical structures, highlight the focal information, and delete irrelevant content. Optimize for information that must be understood quickly rather than a figure that can be studied repeatedly.
- **p. 40:** For formal chemistry presentations, redraw chemical structures in ChemDraw or an equivalent editor instead of using low-quality screenshots.

## Speaking and Timing

- **p. 42:** Respect the time limit and reserve discussion. For a fixed talk slot, finish slightly early; when talk and questions share a slot, leave 2–3 minutes when feasible. Rehearse with timing tools and use concise language.
- **p. 43:** Organize speech before presenting. Define the few points to say on each slide and use visible elements as prompts.
- **pp. 44–46:** Compress repetitive statements into parallel constructions, replace several redundant sentences with one precise sentence, and use transition phrases to make relationships explicit.

## Lessons from the Worked Example

- **p. 48:** A good literature seminar finds sources containing actual answers or data and responds accurately to the assigned guiding questions.
- **pp. 49–50:** Open by defining the scientific problem, explaining why it is difficult, and classifying the relevant systems. This creates a conceptual map before details.
- **pp. 51–72:** Build the report as an evidence sequence. Introduce the background for each case, state what question a method answers, show the decisive observation, connect data to structural/mechanistic inference, compare multiple methods, and progress from historical evidence to modern research and remaining opportunities.
- **pp. 53–71:** The example repeatedly labels the function of evidence—such as how spectroscopy identifies a species, which observation is decisive, and how theory matches experiment. This makes the reasoning visible rather than merely displaying figures.
- **p. 72:** The sample ends with thanks and an invitation for criticism.

## Evaluation Rubric and Examples

- **p. 73:** Evaluate content by logical clarity, appropriate depth, correct causality, comprehensive coverage, and PPT standards. Evaluate delivery by concise language, emphasis, preparation, and timing. Evaluate answers by understanding, accuracy, concision, and prepared backup slides.
- **p. 74:** Positive logical structure uses reasonable categories, separates common principles from special cases, and reaches accurate conclusions.
- **p. 75:** A causal error plus accumulated prose is a serious failure even when the slide looks full.
- **p. 76:** Comprehensive content is informative, properly classified, and easy to compare.
- **p. 77:** Emphasis requires labels, key data, and a conclusion that lets viewers identify the point quickly.
- **p. 78:** A figure without explanatory text, key data, or conclusion fails to communicate its priority.
- **p. 79:** A polished layout keeps text and scientific objects at comparable visual scales, uses consistent arrows, and distributes content evenly.
- **p. 80:** Unbalanced composition, inconsistent pasted structures, and meaningless whitespace reduce clarity and professionalism.
- **p. 81:** Overfilled pages force elements too small, erase hierarchy, and leave the audience unsure where to look.
- **p. 82:** Excessive prose turns the slide into a script; the spoken manuscript should not be pasted into the deck.
- **p. 83:** Numerous screenshots or crops often distort images and text and create inconsistent, unattractive pages.

## Source-Derived Summary

The course's strongest transferable rule can be paraphrased as follows: select content by the assigned scientific questions, arrange it as an explicit chain of reasoning, and redesign every figure so the audience can see the key evidence and conclusion within the brief time available.

Skill-author application: retain a scientific conclusion during Q&A, combine or move acknowledgments when appropriate, and operationalize the course principles as a claim-level evidence contract, route-specific workflows, and quality gates.
